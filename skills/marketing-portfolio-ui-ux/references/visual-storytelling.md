# Visual Storytelling for Marketing and Portfolio Pages

Visual storytelling is the deliberate order of message, proof, emphasis, and action across a page or a connected set of public pages. It should make the next section or route feel earned and understandable, not merely different.

## Build the narrative

1. Start with orientation: what is being presented, for whom, and why it matters.
2. Add explanation or demonstration before asking the visitor to believe a large claim.
3. Place proof close to the proposition it supports.
4. Use deeper detail for visitors who need it; do not force every visitor through the same amount of content.
5. End with a relevant, low-friction next step.

For a longer page, treat sections as chapters with distinct jobs. Use a composition change when the content, evidence, or decision changes. A recurring mark, image treatment, phrase, or shape can connect chapters when it comes from the product's own story; define its meaning and vary its role instead of repeating it as wallpaper.

For a page family, decide what the visitor should carry from one route to another: the chosen option, relevant context, progress, or a clear return path. Carry only context the product actually supports. Keep the core visual identity recognizable while giving detail, form, confirmation, and help pages a layout appropriate to their task. See [Creative Public Experiences](creative-public-experiences.md) for route roles and review guidance.

## Create rhythm without template repetition

Vary emphasis through content importance, not decoration. A page can alternate between concise text, a focused visual, a proof moment, and a detailed section while remaining visually coherent through shared type, spacing, color, and motion rules.

Use whitespace as a pause, not filler. Use contrast to signal a genuine shift in topic or importance. Keep the primary narrative legible when imagery fails to load, animation is skipped, or a visitor enters on a supporting page. Make the main action available without requiring a visitor to watch an intro or complete a scroll sequence.

## Motion and interaction

Use motion to reveal relationships, confirm interaction, or guide attention to a meaningful change. Keep it brief, interruptible, and secondary to content. Avoid scroll hijacking, delayed essential copy, autoplay that competes with reading, or effects that make the page feel slower than it is.

## Review prompts

- Can a first-time visitor explain the page's value after the first viewport?
- Does each section add new evidence, understanding, or choice?
- Are project visuals annotated enough to communicate contribution?
- Is the primary action clear without overpowering the page?
- Would the page still feel coherent with reduced motion and on a narrow screen?
- Does each supporting route answer its own visitor question without breaking the visual and language continuity of the page family?
